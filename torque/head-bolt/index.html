Head Bolt Torque

How cylinder head bolts are specified: staged sequences, torque-to-yield bolts, and why the pattern matters as much as the number.

Short answer: there is no single head bolt torque figure. Head bolts are tightened in stages, in a defined pattern from the centre of the head outward, and most engines built since the 1990s use torque-to-yield (TTY) bolts that finish with an angle — for example 22 ft·lbs, then 90°, then another 90°. TTY bolts are single-use. Always work from the factory sequence for your specific engine.

Why Head Bolts Are Different

The gasket needs even clamp load. A head gasket seals combustion pressure at hundreds of psi across a surface with wildly varying stiffness. If one corner is clamped harder than another, the gasket leaks there first — usually into a coolant passage.

Tightening in stages keeps the head flat. Pulling one bolt to full torque before its neighbours lifts the head at the far end. Every factory sequence runs the whole set to a low value first, then a middle value, then final.

The pattern spirals outward from the centre. Head bolt sequences almost always start at the two centre bolts and work outward in a spiral or alternating pattern, so the head is drawn down from the middle and any distortion is pushed to the edges.

Angle beats torque for accuracy. Thread friction makes a torque reading a poor proxy for clamp load — variation of ±25% is normal. Once a bolt is snug, rotating it by a measured angle stretches it a known amount, which is far more repeatable. That is why modern specs read “30 N·m plus 90°”.

Torque-to-Yield Bolts

They are stretched past their elastic limit on purpose. A TTY bolt is designed to yield slightly at final tightening. That puts it on the flat part of its stress curve, where clamp load stays almost constant even as the head and block expand and contract with heat.

They cannot be reused. A yielded bolt has already given up its stretch. Re-tightening it takes it further along the curve toward fracture, and the clamp load it produces is unpredictable. Replace the full set.

Check length before you condemn a set. Some manufacturers publish a maximum length; a bolt longer than that has stretched and is scrap. If no length is published, treat TTY bolts as single-use without exception.

Buy an angle gauge. Estimating 90° by eye on a socket in a deep well is not accurate enough. A cheap angle gauge or a wrench with a built-in angle mode removes the guesswork.

Preparation That Changes the Result

Clean and chase the block threads. Oil or coolant trapped in a blind hole hydro-locks the bolt: the wrench reads full torque against trapped fluid, not clamp load, and can crack the block. Chase every hole with a thread chaser — not a cutting tap — and blow it out.

Lubricate exactly as specified. Most head bolt specs assume light engine oil on the threads and under the head. Dry or greased threads at the same torque produce a completely different clamp load.

Check the deck and head for flatness. A warped head will not seal at any torque. Check with a straightedge and feeler gauge before the gasket goes anywhere near it.

Follow the retorque instruction, if there is one. Some engines and many aftermarket gaskets call for a retorque after a heat cycle. Others explicitly forbid it. Do what the gasket maker says.

Important Notes

  • • This page explains the method. It does not publish per-engine head bolt figures — those vary by engine family, model year, bolt length and gasket, and a wrong figure here would cost an engine.
  • • Always work from the factory service manual or the gasket manufacturer's instruction sheet for your exact engine.
  • • Never reuse torque-to-yield bolts, and never mix old and new bolts in one head.

Common Questions

What is the torque for head bolts?

There is no universal figure — head bolts are specified per engine as a staged sequence. A typical modern procedure is a snug pass of 20–30 ft·lbs followed by one or two 90° angle stages. Older cast-iron engines with conventional bolts often use three torque passes to a final value in the 65–110 ft·lbs range. Use the factory sequence for your engine.

Can you reuse head bolts?

Conventional head bolts can usually be reused if they are within the manufacturer's length specification and the threads are undamaged. Torque-to-yield bolts cannot — they are deliberately stretched past their elastic limit on final tightening and must be replaced as a complete set.

What is a torque-to-yield head bolt?

A bolt designed to be tightened just past its yield point, so it sits on the flat part of its stress curve where clamp load stays nearly constant through heat cycles. The spec finishes with a rotation angle rather than a torque figure, and the bolt is single-use.

What order do you tighten head bolts in?

Start at the centre of the head and work outward, alternating side to side, in the pattern printed in the service manual. Never work along the head from one end — that lifts the far end and distorts the deck.

Do you oil head bolts before installing them?

Follow the manual. Most specs assume light engine oil on the threads and under the bolt head, and the torque figure is calibrated for that. Installing dry or with assembly grease at the same torque gives a clamp load that is far too low or far too high.
